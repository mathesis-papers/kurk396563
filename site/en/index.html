Corrected formulas and integer exponents for Amdeberhan–Ekhad (2022): Kurkov's OEIS A396563 FORMULA conjecture, revision 15 (1 June 2026)

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The manuscript asks whether complicated alternating sums can be understood through determinants of Stirling numbers, and whether the same explanation works when powers become reciprocals. Ma and Zhang give such an explanation using weighted rectangular fillings, correct two earlier determinant formulas, and present proofs of two conjectured sums recorded by M. Kurkov in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.

Unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind are the coefficients obtained by expanding the product x(x+1)…(x+v−1); write c(v,j) for the coefficient of xʲ. The authors study a square matrix whose entry in row i and column j is c(n+m+i,n+j), with i and j running from 1 to k. Its determinant is called B(n,k,m). Here n and k are nonnegative integers and m is positive; an empty determinant equals one. Kurkov’s A396563 conjecture, in the specified revision of 1 June 2026, concerns an explicit nested sum for the case m=2. The broader question is why that sum’s signs and factorials reproduce this determinant.

The structural answer uses a rectangle with m rows and k columns. Fill its boxes with numbers from 1 through N, where N=n+m, allowing weak increases across rows and requiring strict increases down columns. Give each filling the weight obtained by multiplying all its entries, then add these weights. This is the rectangular Schur polynomial evaluated at 1,…,N, and the authors show that it equals B(n,k,m). The determinant-to-symmetric-polynomial reduction comes from Merca’s 2014 work; Amdeberhan and Ekhad explicitly gave the Schur evaluation in 2022. The manuscript’s contribution is not discovering that evaluation, but correcting and normalizing the subset expansion and extending its exponent range.

The proof first rewrites each matrix entry as a coefficient of a common polynomial times another polynomial of increasing degree. Those additional polynomials have leading coefficient one, so replacing them by ordinary powers changes the basis through a triangular matrix with determinant one. The resulting determinant involves a fixed list of numbers and becomes the rectangular filling sum through a classical Schur identity. A second determinant representation is then expanded by choosing m numbers from that list. Cancellation leaves products of powers divided by differences between chosen and unchosen numbers. Rewriting these denominators produces squared pairwise differences and factorials. Finally, the substitution aₜ=n−jₜ+t turns increasing selections into the decreasing chains of indices in Kurkov’s sum, explaining its precise signs and normalization.

A small example shows why the correction matters. With n=m=k=1, the determinant is c(3,2)=3. The earlier printed subset weights do not cover the full list of available numbers; extending them by zero gives 1 instead. The corrected contributions are −1 and 4, giving 3. The authors also correct the list used in the earlier second-kind Stirling formula, where second-kind numbers count partitions into nonempty blocks. Their example requires S(4,2)=7, whereas the printed list gives a Schur value of 25. Separately, they explain a missing factorial in a companion exponential generating function and an index shift behind another recorded formula.

The main extension replaces the exponent n+k in the normalized sum by any integer a. For n,m≥1, exponents a≥n give the same rectangular Schur evaluation with width a−n. Every integer in −m<a<n gives zero: in the determinant representation, two rows have identical exponents and therefore coincide. For a=−m−r with r≥0, the answer is the Schur evaluation for a rectangle with n rows and r columns, multiplied by (−1)ⁿᵐ and divided by (N!)ᵐ⁺ʳ. Taking reciprocals of the variables and exchanging complementary rectangles explains this branch. The case n=0 is different: the answer is always (m!)ᵃ. After restoring its prefactors, a=−m−1 yields Kurkov’s A132393 conjecture of 24 May 2026.

These representations also explain positivity and boundary values: one permitted filling contributes (m!)ᵏ, so B(n,k,m) is a positive integer at least that large, with equality when n=0. For fixed n and m, the subset expansion makes the sequence in k a finite combination of exponential sequences, recovering the earlier rational generating functions and giving a recurrence, not necessarily of minimal order. A further consequence expresses B as a signed m-by-m determinant of second-kind Stirling numbers. The authors also revisit established companion recurrences and weighted sums with explicit credit to earlier work.

The manuscript supplies written all-parameter arguments, distinct from its reported finite exact-arithmetic comparisons. It describes two Lean declarations covering precisely the original nested-sum identity and the reciprocal-power conjecture, not the entire Schur interpretation, integer-exponent extension or collection of consequences; this guide does not independently check those declarations. The significance is a unified explanation of cancellation and reciprocal powers, with corrected formulas and preserved attribution. The scope is finite algebraic identities, including the stated empty-product extension at m=0. Other transforms, analytic series evaluations and beta-function identities remain outside its coverage, rather than being settled here.
